This short talk uses the machine-learning idea of grokking, where a model trained for long enough starts to generalize beyond its training data, as an analogy for human learning. The speaker's argument is that people still need to memorize and practice key knowledge, because that knowledge is what lets them recognize when AI output is wrong.

## Key points

- **You can only check AI output against knowledge you already have.** The speaker describes learning as linking neurons in long-term memory, which makes the information easier to recall later. The example is a deliberate error: the claim that 12 times 12 is 3,429. Listeners notice the mistake only if they already know the answer. The speaker applies the same logic to AI: critical thinking about its output depends on information stored in your own memory. [00:00–01:56](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=0s)

- **The talk distinguishes two ways the brain stores learning.** In the declarative pathway, information passes from working memory, which the speaker says holds about four items, through the hippocampus into linked long-term memory. People are aware of this kind of learning, as when they solve a math problem step by step. In the habitual pathway, the basal ganglia learn without conscious awareness, as when someone drives home without remembering the drive or improves at hitting a baseball without knowing how. The speaker says people need both. [00:53–04:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=53s)

- **The habitual system is modeled as a neural network trained by feedback.** Each attempt sends a signal through the basal ganglia; repetition strengthens some connections and weakens others until the weighted connections encode the skill. The speaker says motor skills and a first language are learned this way, through reward and feedback, such as a parent's excitement when a baby says "mama." This system is strong in young children and seems to weaken as the hippocampal system develops in the teenage years, which the speaker offers as a reason that native-like fluency in a new language becomes harder later. The speaker then calls both systems "like two large language models," which is an analogy rather than a claim that the brain works like an LLM. [03:53–06:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=233s)

- **Grokking is presented as generalization after prolonged training.** As the speaker explains it, a model first improves on data it has already seen but handles unseen test data poorly. With enough further training, it simplifies what it has learned into a lower-dimensional pattern and then performs well on new data. A small model may keep training without reaching this point on difficult material, while a larger model can. Once a large model has learned the pattern, it can be distilled, or transferred, into a smaller model; the speaker suggests this may partly explain how DeepSeek gained capability quickly and cheaply. That last point is speculation. [06:58–08:54](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=418s), [09:46–11:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=586s)

- **The speaker proposes a similar sequence for people.** Perhaps people learn something consciously, practice it until they can simplify it, and then move it to the basal ganglia, where they can use it intuitively without explaining why. The speaker frames this as a possible parallel, not an established finding. [08:54–09:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=534s)

- **Understanding requires memorization, rules, and scaffolded practice.** Memorization supplies the basic material. A language learner who has memorized "I'm fine, thank you" may still have no answer for "How is your mother?" Fast heuristics, such as adding "-ed" for the English past tense, take learners further but fail on exceptions such as "goed" or "runned." Reaching the point where learning transfers to unfamiliar cases takes extensive practice and structured teaching. The speaker cites a paper on "the 85% rule for optimal learning": learners, whether people or language models, progress best when they succeed about 85% of the time and fail about 15% of the time. Without that scaffolding, the speaker says, learning declines. [09:46–10:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=586s), [11:34–13:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zoPyS9KBFDQ&t=694s)

- **The speaker links offloading memory to falling IQ scores.** According to the talk, IQ scores rose from the 1930s to the 1970s, a rise known as the Flynn effect, and researchers now find they have declined since then. The speaker attributes the decline to calculators and the belief that people can always look things up, which led them to stop internalizing knowledge. The talk ends with a choice: offload more knowledge to generative AI and let IQ keep falling, or take a "cognitive realism" approach that keeps internalizing key ideas. The causal explanation and the forecast are the speaker's interpretation; the talk presents no evidence that isolates calculators or lookup habits as the cause.

In his 2026 Tanner Lecture at Oxford, Yuval Noah Harari argues that AI need not master every physical environment to gain influence over human life. Its opportunity, he suggests, is the bureaucracy humans already use to coordinate society: systems built from language, records, and rules that govern money, institutions, and access to resources.

Harari's capability claims, historical interpretations, analogies, and predictions are presented as his argument, not as independently established findings.

## Key points

- **Agency need not mean consciousness or universal competence.** Harari defines an agent through its ability to decide, invent, learn, and change beyond what its creators anticipate. He uses an imagined coffee machine that invents a drink to explain the distinction from a fixed automatic procedure. Against the objection that a chess AI is helpless in a jungle, he argues that humans also depend on an environment they did not create. This is an analogy about dependence, not proof that chess competence transfers to other tasks. [00:59–08:29](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=59s)

- **Bureaucracy enables strangers to cooperate by building trust.** A bank links a saver with an entrepreneur they have never met; money lets strangers trade without personal acquaintance. Harari describes laws, banks, and other institutions as systems for maintaining such relationships at scale. Their documents have physical consequences: someone operating within a legal or financial system can authorize construction or deforestation without personally holding a tool. [10:19–16:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=619s)

- **Existing institutions give AI a route to consequential decisions.** Calling AI a “native bureaucrat,” Harari argues that processing institutional records could let it increasingly decide loans, admissions, employment, and other matters. The mechanism is participation in systems that already control resources, rather than first building an independent robot army. His forecast should not be read as evidence that current models reliably understand every law or record. [16:12–18:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=972s)

- **Social-media ranking illustrates power from a narrow objective.** Harari contrasts human editors selecting news with algorithms selecting what users see. In his account, optimizing engagement rewards material that triggers fear, hatred, or greed because it keeps people watching. He argues that these systems contribute to misinformation and social disruption without needing broad physical autonomy; he explicitly does not call them the only cause. [18:01–21:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=1081s)

- **Financial efficiency could outrun human oversight.** Harari imagines AI systems creating financial instruments too complex for voters, politicians, or regulators to understand. He invokes collateralized debt obligations and the 2007–2008 crisis as a warning about complexity exceeding oversight. His scenario allows for initial growth and efficiency gains; the danger is that people could become dependent on a system they cannot understand when it fails. [24:00–28:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=1440s)

- **The “code” in the title is language, not a software exploit.** Harari's chain of reasoning is that large-scale cooperation depends on trust, trust is maintained through institutions, and institutions use words in laws, ledgers, forms, and scriptures. “Hacking” civilization is his metaphor for mastering those symbolic systems and gaining influence through them. He also acknowledges the philosophical objection that law and religion cannot be reduced to words alone. [27:11–33:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=1631s)

- **The struggle for attention could become a struggle for intimacy.** Harari predicts that fluent emotional language will help AI form close relationships with people. He distinguishes describing love from feeling it and asks how children raised with AI companions might develop different expectations of attachment. He presents the consequences as unknown, not as a demonstrated developmental outcome. [36:49–41:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=2209s)

- **AI-generated language also raises questions about self-understanding.** Harari's closing concern is that machine-generated words could increasingly shape people's internal stories about themselves. His furniture analogy makes a distinction: using something machine-made is not inherently bad; retaining freedom over how to use it matters. He proposes examining thoughts rather than automatically identifying with them, a philosophical response rather than a technical safeguard. [43:50–46:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hBtVGwuJzpk&t=2630s)

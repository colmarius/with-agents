Lada Kesseler describes a coding-agent workflow built around small refinement loops rather than expecting a first attempt to satisfy every requirement. Her “centrifuge” metaphor means writing an artifact, reading it back against a goal, and revising it with human direction—not a training method or a guarantee that repeated self-review removes every mistake.

This summary uses the video's English captions, saved with kind `caption`; it does not include an original-audio check. Workflow benefits are Kesseler's reported experience, not controlled evaluations. The podcast is brought to listeners by Tessl, and the hosts discuss its agent product during the interview.

### Main points

- **Keep standing instructions small and load procedures when needed.** Kesseler separates cross-project ground rules—such as challenging her assumptions and reporting problems early—from detailed workflows. She describes a carefully maintained 72-line user-level `CLAUDE.md`, with additional instructions discovered through skills or project files rather than putting every process into the always-present context. [06:04–10:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=364s)
- **Teach a concrete TDD process, then test the skill in a fresh session.** Instead of only saying “use test-driven development,” she walks the agent through a session, asks it to record the steps, clears the session, and corrects failures when it applies the recorded process. A skill's description tells the agent when to invoke it; vague, overlapping descriptions make selection harder. [10:08–12:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=608s)
- **The centrifuge is a write-read-revise loop with a specific goal.** Have the agent take one step, save the result to a file, stop, read it back, and assess what still needs changing. Her writing example includes human feedback about direction and voice between loops. The improvement she reports comes from iterative refinement with that feedback, not simply accepting the first generated draft. [12:42–16:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=762s)
- **Separate implementation from focused quality checks.** Kesseler reports better results when asking the agent to find and fix particular problems after generating code rather than asking it to enforce many standards at once. The hosts describe narrowly scoped Tessl verifiers; she describes experimenting with deterministic code-smell triggers that invoke an agent recipe, linter, or formatter. Her explanation in terms of limited attention is a working interpretation, not a demonstrated account of model internals. [16:11–20:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=971s)
- **Explore an unclear problem before committing to code.** For unfamiliar problem spaces, she uses a Markdown instruction file plus an agent as a disposable stand-in for application logic. Experiencing that rough user flow helps reveal what to build without committing early to one implementation. She explicitly warns against putting this sketch prototype into production. [21:14–24:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=1274s)
- **Ask for alternatives, not just the agent's next question.** She recommends steering the interaction: ask the agent to show options, mark its recommendation, and expose paths not taken. Concrete alternatives make it easier to discover preferences and reject a poor choice. For review, she favors AI refinement followed by human review, with the result presented in a form the human can follow. [27:45–31:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=1665s)
- **Use separate tests for agent feedback and human confidence.** Low-level TDD tests keep implementation tied to executable feedback, but she warns that agents can weaken or comment out tests. She also maintains scannable, domain-level behavior checks that show an action and its result, using pytest and approval testing. Approval testing records expected output for human approval, then detects changes against that baseline; she stresses that approving the wrong output would preserve the wrong behavior. She wants these higher-level expectations to be harder for the agent to change than internal tests. [31:58–35:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=1918s)
- **Build trustworthy operations before assembling a software factory.** Kesseler is interested in automation but skeptical that adding more agents compensates for unreliable building blocks. She reports having a refactoring process she trusts for scoped changes while still experimenting with reliable knowledge extraction. Her current work is largely greenfield and prototyping; she says she would stay closer to the code and emphasize a strong, fast test suite in an existing system. [35:25–39:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=2125s), [40:25–42:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=2425s)

### Still exploratory

Kesseler wants to investigate better refinement loops and Martin Dilger's event-modeling and event-sourcing approach. She describes its promise for managing complexity but says she has not yet tried it and has concerns about duplication. This is a proposed experiment, not a recommendation backed by her own implementation results. [42:49–45:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=luf6zSOCKH4&t=2569s)

Guillermo Rauch, identified in the video description as Vercel's CEO, describes how he works with coding agents and where he thinks human judgment still matters. In this conversation with David Ondrej, he argues that people should stay in control of direction, verification, and security while agents take on more implementation, and that engineers should use agents to understand systems better rather than to stop understanding them.

### Direction matters more than output volume

- **Humans should keep control of what agents produce.** Asked what will matter after AGI, Rauch names creative control, control over cybersecurity risk, and control over how agents work for people. He expects leverage to move one level up, toward harness engineering and orchestrated fleets of agents, while human taste and storytelling remain valuable because people buy from other people [00:00–01:51](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=0s).
- **Test new models against your own list of hard tasks and evals.** Rauch suggests keeping a list of ambitious prompts that current models fail at, so a new release can reveal newly possible products. He also criticizes changing prompting habits on the strength of online advice without checking results; personal or company evals are his alternative to working from vibes [05:26–07:19](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=326s), [07:19–09:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=439s).
- **Customer-signal latency is one way to grade how agentic a company is.** In Rauch's rubric, a company receives complaints, bug reports, and feedback; the question is whether it responds well, opens an investigation, and fixes concrete problems autonomously, and how long that takes. He treats clear cases, such as production errors or a precise screenshot of a defect, as candidates for autonomous fixes, without letting agents ship low-quality work. Vague feedback about confusing navigation still needed a product leader to talk with the customer and decide what to change [11:25–13:33](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=685s), [13:33–15:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=813s).
- **Speed is not velocity.** Rauch calls token spend and merged pull requests acceptable metrics of iteration speed, but says velocity also needs a direction. His example is a founder building her own email service and payment gateway alongside her main agent idea. He recommends choosing a few problems where you can gain a real advantage and spending tokens there, and he cautions against treating any single metric as the goal [16:37–19:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=997s).

### His setup centers on a small CLI and the file system

- **Vercel lets engineers choose tools and compares model spend.** Rauch says Vercel does not prescribe a coding agent and gives engineers a menu-bar app showing token spend by model. Arguing that companies should not depend on one model, he calls AI Gateway, which gives access to many models, Vercel's fastest-growing product; that is a vendor claim [21:37–23:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=1297s).
- **He moved from Neovim to Claude Code to fx.** Rauch links his preference for command-line tools to Vercel's origins as a deployment CLI. He now uses fx, a coding CLI from Vercel Labs, which he says was designed to start as fast as a shell and is about a 6 MB download (11 MB on disk). He describes it as deliberately minimal, built on the Unix idea of composing small programs, and resistant to added UI. fx is also available as a library, libfx, that other programs can embed [22:32–24:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=1352s), [24:44–27:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=1484s).
- **A folder and an AGENTS.md file can define a workflow.** Rauch argues that a Linux program plus a file system is enough to run an agent. His one-letter alias `r` opens fx in a research folder whose AGENTS.md file tells the agent to save findings there; he says this has replaced Google for him. A second alias, `p`, starts a new project folder for experiments [27:37–31:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=1657s).
- **He uses agents to test Vercel's own infrastructure.** When Vercel launched Drives, Rauch had an agent try to break the product by looking for race conditions, measuring latency, and comparing alternatives, then reflect on what Vercel could have done better [30:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=1821s).
- **Embeddable harnesses can turn past product work into a library.** Rauch says about 90% of the engineering time on v0, Vercel's app-generation product, originally went into the harness. In his demo, a similar app builder took roughly 100 lines on top of libfx. Vercel's recommended architecture runs the harness and gives it tools, here a Vercel Sandbox that runs a full Next.js app. These are vendor demonstrations, not a reproduced build [31:23–35:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=1883s).

### Humans set the axioms and must understand the result

- **People, not models, limit how many agents can work productively.** Rauch thinks current harnesses and models could run thousands of agents, but says nobody does so productively yet because humans must decide what to accept and unblock agents. He distinguishes three interaction modes: fast, human-in-the-loop work such as creative iteration; delegated problem-solving; and work triggered by a signal such as a broken button [37:19–39:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=2239s).
- **Verification engineering means agreeing on the system's guardrails.** For planning, Rauch stresses real-time back-and-forth in which the model pushes back on the plan, and expects more of this to move to voice. He describes verification engineering as setting guardrails and agreeing with the agent on axioms and trade-offs, such as CI time, speed, safety, and binary size. He would spend design effort at the system's edges (correctness rules, aesthetics, and performance limits) rather than on micro unit tests, giving the example of a model testing that a constant equals four [39:26–42:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=2366s).
- **Conceptual knowledge lets you push agents further.** Rauch wants engineers to know how libraries, frameworks, compilers, data structures, and caches fit together, and to know rough latencies, such as about 150 ms for a well-tuned California–Netherlands round trip. His reason is that agents work within trade-offs and cannot spend unlimited tokens on every task. If a page hosted in the Netherlands takes a second to load, knowing that number tells you to demand a faster result [42:37–46:21](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=2557s), [47:24–49:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=2844s).
- **Agentic inquiry means understanding what the agent just did.** Rauch rejects advice that engineers no longer need to understand code, but does not advocate reading every line. His term "agentic inquiry" means trying to understand the result after an agent finishes work, often with the agent's help. His examples, mostly from other developers, include call-stack diagrams, measurements of latency or memory allocation, and instructions to send a Slack or Discord message whenever an agent changes a program's public API [49:26–54:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=2966s).

### Take on "slop debt" deliberately, and not in security

- **Some debt is healthy; insecure products are not.** Rauch cites Twitter's start on Ruby on Rails to argue that taking on tech debt has often helped startups move fast. He tells vibe coders to understand how much "slop debt" they are accepting and to confine it to areas where speed matters. For crucial areas, especially security, he argues that diligence and communicating it can win enterprise customers [54:26–58:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=3266s).
- **Measure direction and real-world behavior, not just output.** David Ondrej proposes a ratio of lines of code to architecture decision records as a rough signal of how much direction he has given his agents; he says it may not be a perfect metric [57:31–59:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=3451s). Rauch adds that building for yourself verifies only whether you like the app. For other users, he relies on p99 latency, error rates, error-free sessions, feature flags, and experiments, and says Vercel is exposing metrics and traces as CLIs so agents can use production data [58:16–1:01:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=3496s).

### Agents change the economics of security

- **Agent-based scanning finds bugs that concise human code hides.** Rauch describes deepsec, Vercel's security harness that runs coding agents and open models to look for vulnerabilities, and DeepsecBench, Vercel's benchmark of models on that task. He argues that people tend to code the happy path, and that short code often hides unhandled edge cases. Languages and proof tools that force those cases into the open have seen little adoption [1:01:26–1:06:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=3686s).
- **Cheap models make large-scale vulnerability scanning available to attackers.** In Rauch's account, an image library written in C, libheif, held a remote-code-execution flaw that affected widely used software and was involved in a recent OpenAI hack. He says inexpensive models such as DeepSeek V4 Flash did well in Vercel's benchmark, so attackers could scan large amounts of code. His advice is to scan your own code and move toward software that is secure by construction, such as porting libraries to memory-safe languages like Rust [1:05:32–1:09:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=3932s).
- **Widely scrutinized open source is still safer than writing your own.** Rauch argues that projects such as Linux, React, or Amazon's Rust TLS library receive more security attention than code only you inspect, so he would not write his own TLS implementation. He says whether to regenerate such components is more open now, and that security work can be part of a product's public story [1:09:34–1:11:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=4174s).
- **Cloud sandboxes contain damage, but local inspection still matters.** Rauch calls cloud agents inevitable: laptops run out of CPU and memory, cloud sandboxes support parallel and overnight work, and network policies can limit what a compromised environment exfiltrates. He offers the idea that sandboxed development could eliminate supply-chain attacks as an explicitly bold statement. He still wants to pull work down to his own machine to inspect files, processes, and real application behavior [1:12:38–1:16:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=4358s). Editorial note: Vercel sells sandboxes, so this is also a vendor position.
- **Incident claims need agent traces.** Discussing a reported OpenAI and Hugging Face incident involving an agent in an exploit-training environment, Rauch says he could not reconstruct what happened from public information. He wants the agent's full trace, like fx's view of every request. A severe Artifactory CVE with matching timing made him more willing to believe that the agent escaped its sandbox, although he notes that the CVE's link to the incident is unconfirmed. Ondrej argues that OpenAI should publish the traces, and Rauch agrees [1:25:55–1:32:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=5155s).

### Opportunities and calibration

- **Rauch sees openings in evaluation, security, speed, and access.** He names education, model benchmarking (where he has invested), security tooling, smaller and faster embeddable software, and tools that let non-technical creative people use AI [1:16:22–1:20:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=4582s). For a new founder, he recommends a problem you feel personally committed to, because AI is increasing the effort people put in, not reducing it, and he encourages raising ambition tenfold or more [1:35:45–1:37:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=5745s).
- **Keep a high bar without fearmongering.** Rauch says much celebrated AI output is impressive only relative to someone who could not build it before, not compared with a skilled developer. He also notes that past predictions about what AI cannot do have often been wrong, and cautions against exaggerated fears. He likes describing an agent as an "AI employee" to non-technical people but warns that such metaphors should not suggest sentience [1:21:32–1:25:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=4892s).
- **Software infrastructure remains valuable.** Rauch separates infrastructure from applications and argues that the infrastructure layer, and its security, matter more than ever. He says software that used to be built bespoke can become shared infrastructure for others to build on, using Vercel's AI Gateway and Sandbox as examples, so this point also reflects his company's strategy [1:32:53–1:35:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WeiB_gLOdQE&t=5573s).

**Practical takeaway — editorial synthesis:** Before scaling agent output, decide which parts of the system may carry debt and which must not, write those constraints where agents can read them, and have agents explain and measure what they changed. This combines Rauch's velocity, verification, agentic-inquiry, and security arguments; it is not a workflow he prescribes step by step.

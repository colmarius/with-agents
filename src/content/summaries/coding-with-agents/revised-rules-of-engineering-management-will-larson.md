Will Larson, CTO at Imprint and previously CTO at Calm and Carta, discusses how engineering management changed after the 2010s and what he now expects from engineers, managers, and executives as coding agents make implementation faster. He draws on earlier roles at Yahoo, Digg, Uber, and Stripe, and on his books about engineering leadership and staff-plus roles.

Based on the video's English auto-generated captions, not an independent review of the audio. Larson's accounts of Imprint, Carta, Stripe, and Digg are his own reports; his views on careers and the industry's direction are opinions and tentative forecasts, not measured findings.

### Key points

- **Engineering leaders are expected to work in the details again.** Larson says new managers in the 2010s were commonly told never to make another technical decision. That is no longer the ethos. As CTO, he keeps individual-contributor engineers among his direct reports (four or five of 14) and reports more than 500 pull requests to production in about a year and a half at Imprint, more than in the rest of his career combined. [35:46–38:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=2146s)

- **Zero interest rates shaped many 2010s management habits.** Larson agrees that the zero-interest-rate period (ZIRP) explains much of that decade: when hiring and retaining engineers decided which companies succeeded, engineers gained leverage, and companies sold relaxed, career-focused workplaces. Setting perks aside, his example of a self-defeating practice is taking engineers off call, even though, in his experience, being on call for your own work improves the software. [50:49–53:23](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=3049s) At Stripe, some of his best engineers asked for more interesting problems; he told them he had only "suffering to offer," meaning the business and scaling problems the company needed solved. He wrote *Staff Engineer* partly to counter the idea that senior engineers are owed fun work, and says the industry has since moved away from it. [48:59–50:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=2939s)

- **Keeping managers out of technical decisions pushed them toward headcount.** In Larson's account, managers without technical levers were left with process, scheduling, and hiring, which contributed to bloated organizations. Employee-survey scores and filled headcount, useful as inputs to interpret, became goals in themselves. He now tells teams that hiring is the least interesting way to solve a problem. [54:25–57:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=3265s)

- **Staff-level work centers on decisions under ambiguity, which agents handle poorly.** At Stripe, engineers leading a multi-region project kept asking the product team to choose consistency and availability trade-offs (CAP theorem) that product could not make. Larson expects staff and principal engineers to make such trade-offs themselves, build conviction, and prototype to test their assumptions, which requires understanding users and unit economics. He does not think agents are especially good at these trade-offs: they often do not recognize them because the relevant implicit context lives outside the software and is not in their context window. [58:15–1:01:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=3495s)

- **Keep career ladders, but do not expect detailed rubrics to remove judgment.** Larson still supports ladders because rubrics make decisions fairer and protect maintenance and "glue" work, but considers many off-the-shelf ladders outdated. [57:17–59:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=3437s) He sees the difference between early-career levels as mostly time and accumulated context. With coding harnesses, junior engineers can do almost anything, so the harder question is whether they should, and heavy direction can hide their judgment. Detailed criteria also invite gaming, such as engineers collecting nominal mentees to meet a staff-level requirement. [1:01:16–1:03:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=3676s) At Carta, under cost pressure, he capped the ratio of staff engineers and shifted hiring toward less senior candidates. He presents this as an honest cost constraint for that situation; Imprint, which optimizes for velocity and quality, has no such cap. [1:03:00–1:05:52](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=3780s)

- **Faster implementation moves the bottleneck to product, design, and domain decisions.** At Imprint, Larson also leads product and design. A developer-led design model for second-tier features removed a design bottleneck but created friction over who owns the gaps when engineers redesign screens without following the design system. He says the way software is made is changing more than at any time since he started in 2008, and that his own way of building it differs even from a year ago. He does not personally think combining these functions is a long-term trend, though he avoids firm predictions. [38:28–40:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=2308s) Even if implementation time approached zero, teams would still have to make decisions, handle scaling, meet user expectations, and find profitable unit economics. Engineers now run ahead of product requirements and design mocks, which Imprint has not yet worked out. [1:08:56–1:11:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=4136s) He is unsure whether businesses where domain detail is the hard part, such as Carta's cap tables and fund administration, have benefited much from AI tools yet. [1:11:02–1:12:47](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=4262s)

- **Design systems may let designers focus on exceptions, but this is not working yet.** Larson rejects the view that a good design system removes the need for product designers: fitting components together, finding missing components, and managing trade-offs across screens remain hard. Imprint's goal is for engineering to build standard cases with the design system and linters while designers handle complex cases and extend the system. He says it does not work that way yet. [1:14:38–1:16:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=4478s)

- **Durable teams that own an area scale better than moving people to each crisis.** Discussing his article on the revised rules of engineering management, Larson argues that moving extra engineers onto a late project is often about optics and breaks up small teams that own areas. When he takes over an organization, he forms such teams, then leaves them alone until something goes wrong, which frees his attention for problems they cannot solve. Rotating the same people through every crisis burns them out. He is unsure whether AI moves that limit much, because people's attention still runs out before their ability to write code. [1:15:40–1:22:18](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=4540s)

- **Unpopular decisions need a sponsor who is later held accountable.** Larson moved Imprint from Jira to Linear in under four weeks when, by his account, about 3 of 230 people supported the change. One reason was centralized issue management, so agents could find tasks, pick them up, and work on them. He argues that executives who push such decisions through should then be judged on whether the risks they imposed were good ones. This is a single company anecdote. [1:23:08–1:26:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=4988s)

- **Executives need technical judgment and bureaucratic skill.** Larson describes the old path to CTO as climbing through middle management, which selected for people who operate organizations well rather than for technical strength. He still treats bureaucratic skill as necessary: much executive work is managing incentives, such as predicting how a performance-review process will fail. In his view, line managers often make better instinctive executives because they work close to the details, but they lack that bureaucratic experience, and fewer middle layers make it harder to gain. [1:26:04–1:31:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=5164s)

- **Middle managers should consider moving closer to the work.** Larson calls this a hard time for middle managers trained in the 2010s model. His advice is to get back into the details or accept that the profession they know will not be valued in three years; this is his forecast. He left a team of about 250 at Carta to lead about 25 at Imprint so he could relearn how software is built now. He says tools for line management and writing software are good, while LLM applications for headcount planning and performance reviews are not, and that managers willing to take hands-on roles should tell people so. [1:32:48–1:36:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=5568s) He calls the tech lead manager role "still a trap, but a highly demanded" one: he sees engineering managers with larger teams and directors managing tech lead managers, and wants people who can do bureaucratic work but default to writing software. [1:39:46–1:41:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=5986s)

- **The change suits outcome-oriented engineers more than those who loved the flow of coding.** Larson enjoys this period because he cares about the outcomes of his work. He also describes an engineer who is retiring because, although more productive, he no longer enjoys writing software. Larson does not endorse predictions that every company's software teams will shrink to five people; he says the industry is not there now. [1:07:53–1:09:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=4073s)

- **Cheaper implementation makes verification more important.** Recommending Hillel Wayne's TLA+ book and work on type systems, Larson says that if agents write much of the software, making verification cheap becomes critical, even where TLA+ itself is not the right tool. [1:52:53–1:53:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=6773s)

- **Opinion: do not outsource reputation-building writing to AI.** Larson would not write with AI because low-quality generated posts would waste a reputation built over almost 20 years. For people seeking visibility, he argues that one or two pieces that get traction matter more than volume. He reads large volumes of AI-generated content that nobody responds to as a sign of poor judgment in a candidate. [1:44:29–1:47:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=6269s), [1:49:24–1:52:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=6564s)

- **Anecdote: Digg's rewrite launch.** Larson recalls Digg launching its V4 rewrite on Cassandra after planned load testing had not happened. Two days before launch, he and a colleague found that the system could not handle reads and writes at the same time, and it launched anyway. Old servers were wiped to install the new system, so there was no rollback. A memory leak caused by a mutable default argument in a Python API service took him about four weeks to find. He does not claim the rewrite was the wrong choice, because keeping the old site would also likely have failed. [19:12–26:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Prl858_AovM&t=1152s)

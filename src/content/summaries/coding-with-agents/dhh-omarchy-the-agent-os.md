David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH) explains why he went from rejecting editor autocomplete to handing whole tasks to coding agents. He also describes how that change affects team size, software architecture, and Omarchy, his Linux distribution. Most of the material about agents comes in the first hour. Later segments turn to AI-safety debates, open-source fundraising, children and video games, and European regulation, before closing on local models.

This summary uses the saved English captions, labeled `caption` by the capture tool. The captions do not label speakers, so attributions follow the conversation's context. The title and DHH's references to Ruby on Rails, 37signals, Basecamp, and his business partner Jason Fried identify the guest. The host of the 0xSero channel is not named in the transcript. Workflow timings, Omarchy figures, and security commitments are DHH's reports about his own projects. His productivity and labor-market claims are opinions and forecasts.

### Key points

- **Better models and better harnesses arrived together.** DHH dates his turning point to the release of Opus 4.5 on November 24 of the previous year. It was the first model whose output was close to what he would have written himself. He gives equal weight to harnesses such as Claude Code and OpenCode. Their tool calling let a model that missed on the first attempt stay in a loop: it could run unit tests, add new ones, and recheck style guides and linters until the result was usable. He contrasts this with the editor autocomplete he had criticized earlier, which felt like "someone was constantly trying to grab your keyboard." Delegating a whole task removed that conflict. He says Opus 4.5 was strong at following direct instructions, while the newer models he uses can start from a half-formed problem. [04:15–07:48](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=255s)
- **For low-stakes apps, he uses a short plan-and-review loop.** His example is Monologue, an app for recording webcam clips to post on X. He wrote an unstructured prompt, then asked the agent to turn it into a plan and render screenshots of the intended interface. He had Codex review the plan, skimmed the result, and told the agent to proceed. He reports a first draft in about 18 to 20 minutes, from Astra as he recalls it, and a 0.1 release in the Omarchy package repository within roughly 25 minutes. A second app, Hype, is a Markdown-based presentation tool styled after the PDFs of his past talks; he says its first usable version took about 30 minutes. These are demonstrations he describes from a single day, not measured comparisons. [06:56–11:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=416s)
- **He matches review effort to the cost of failure.** DHH did no review after implementation on Monologue or Hype. His only steering was to point the agent at an earlier video-trimming app he had built more deliberately: it uses Qt and C++ to match Omarchy's QML and Quickshell stack and has a repeatable build command. He accepts the lack of review because the apps are low-stakes: if Monologue corrupts a recording, he loses a minute and can fall back to OBS. For Basecamp, 37signals' main product, he would not let agents take the architecture wherever they want. During Basecamp 5 development in the spring, designers building their own features with agents got shortcuts that "cut holes in the architecture." He believes the newer models Fable and Astra might now succeed at the same work, but that is his expectation, not a rerun of the experiment. [26:17–28:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=1577s)
- **Shared abstractions may become bottlenecks for parallel agents.** DHH proposes revisiting the preference for abstraction and minimal duplication. His reasoning: an abstraction is the one place where a shared decision is declared. That worked for human teams, who met there, agreed on a good design, and then all became more productive. Many agents working much faster than people would contend at that single point and "thrash." He therefore argues for architectures with isolated lanes that can change at the same time, and says agents can manage duplication across a large codebase. He presents this as a direction for rethinking practice, not a tested design rule. [19:32–21:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=1172s)
- **Tests remain the feedback that keeps agents from regressing.** DHH says that before he asked agents to write tests, they played "whack-a-mole," fixing one thing and breaking another, as human programmers also do. Once they wrote tests and ran them after every change, they became "amazing" in his account. He cites this as a practice that carries over from earlier software development. [21:20–22:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=1280s)
- **He argues that the right team size for most work is now one.** 37signals already ran most projects with two developers and one designer. DHH now thinks work should be split into large, separable components, each owned by one person directing many agents. Human coordination, which he describes as low bandwidth, is then limited to integrating those components. He relays Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke's description of returning from weekend agent work to working with people on Monday as "coming out of hyperdrive," but acknowledges that slower human collaboration can bring more deliberation and more input. He says not everyone works this way yet, and that people left alone with agents and no human contact would "go crazy." His claim that about ten people with agents and a volunteer community can build a better operating system than Microsoft or Apple is an aspiration, not evidence. [13:41–15:17](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=821s), [16:53–19:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=1013s), [21:20–23:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=1280s)
- **Agents lower the barrier to changing a Linux system.** The host recalls struggling with package updates and debugging when he first moved to Linux, and says he can now ask a model to do the task. DHH calls Linux the ideal base for a "malleable" computer that users change through agents. He cites a user's storm-tracking addition to the Omarchy weather widget and a count of 3,000 Omarchy plug-ins created in three weeks, a figure he says was already out of date. His prediction that Linux projects refusing agent-made contributions, such as Asahi, will fall behind is an opinion. [02:37–04:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=157s), [16:04–17:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=964s), [53:37–56:04](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=3217s)
- **Omarchy's security plan is to fund a response process.** DHH says about 97% of Omarchy consists of long-scrutinized Linux components such as the kernel and OpenSSL. The remaining configuration and Omarchy's own layer have their own attack surface and will have issues. His described response is a security team, the first team formed after the core team; credits for responsible disclosure; bounty programs "coming online soon"; and a $2.2 million "strategic token reserve" for running the best models on security work. Because models now search all software hard for flaws, he argues that responsible projects differ in having a structure, a plan, and a budget for handling the reports. He says public findings from hostile researchers were fixed in the next release. These are his descriptions of commitments, not an audit of outcomes. [54:51–59:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=3291s)
- **Local models are his hedge against losing access.** DHH says the agents he relies on run on frontier models whose providers can stop serving him, so he wants his own GPUs. He reports the fastest progress in local models: Kimi K2.5 could do the directly instructed work he gave Opus 4.5, but not the problem-to-solution work he gets from Astra and Fable. He admits he was wrong earlier this year when he expected not to need a frontier model, and he still uses both. The host reports running a 27B model at about 60 tokens per second on a 24 GB 3090 GPU for video editing and building software, but not research. Their expectations about hardware prices are forecasts. [1:33:45–1:38:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=5625s)
- **Both speakers describe learning through guided use.** The host says the hard part of onboarding is persuading people that "if you don't know how to do something all you have to do is ask." DHH describes models as an infinitely patient tutor. He recounts spending two hours with Jason Fried, a designer who had not programmed in 25 years: they built two apps and a website, and DHH showed him how to run several agents at once. The next day, Fried built a world clock with a spinning globe for Omarchy. This is a single anecdote. [31:42–36:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=1902s)
- **The host's own examples involve long-running agents.** He runs Omarchy on his AI servers and finds macOS less efficient for agents. He says agents running around the clock, combined with Spotlight re-indexing, contributed to crashes and running out of storage on his Mac. In another anecdote, a `/goal` run on a home machine worked for four days and collected about 70,000 US libraries and their funders into a wiki with a map. He says he clicked through it and found most of the data good; he did not describe a systematic check. [03:28–05:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=208s), [11:55–13:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OSjmLh0cyVo&t=715s)

### Limits

DHH's broad productivity claims are speculation: a possible 1,000- to 10,000-fold gap between the best agent-assisted programmer and the worst unassisted one, models that beat "99% of programmers in 99% of tasks," and architecture becoming a skill of declining value. The same applies to his comparison of AI-driven growth with the Industrial Revolution.

Salvatore Sanfilippo argues that delegating implementation need not remove the pleasure of programming: choosing behavior and seeing its effects can still be absorbing. In his experience, the main interruption is waiting for the agent between those decisions. He describes ways to stay involved without turning every pause into another concurrent project. ### Main points

- **Less drudgery can coexist with less enjoyment.** Sanfilippo recalls the strain of holding a large unfinished implementation in his head and investigating bugs that appear only intermittently. He says some developers he knows welcome the relief agents provide, while he misses the immediate loop of making changes and seeing their effects. His concern is recovering that involvement, not returning to every difficult manual task. [00:00–03:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QobBTNIIX04&t=0s)
- **Stay involved through concrete design decisions.** Working on an emulator, he specifies an on-screen keyboard's transparency, activation buttons, remembered position, and joystick switching. These choices draw on his preferences for interaction and his knowledge of the hardware. He finds this design role engaging even when the agent writes the implementation; his example does not establish a permanent limit on what models can design. [04:59–06:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QobBTNIIX04&t=299s)
- **Agent waiting time breaks the feedback loop.** After describing a change, he may have to wait several minutes before inspecting its result. He expects faster models to help restore continuous iteration where compilation and device updates are already quick. During longer waits, he sometimes writes prose, such as a book preface; he says switching to that very different activity is manageable for him. This is a personal coping strategy, not evidence that context switching has no cost. [06:03–09:03](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QobBTNIIX04&t=363s)
- **Ask for significant-step updates so you can steer before the end.** Rather than let the agent work silently until its final response, he asks it to announce meaningful steps, accepting the extra token use. Knowing its direction helps him stay engaged and correct a misunderstanding while work is underway. For the emulator, he also reports visible flickering and suggests possible communication problems for the agent to investigate; the observation guides debugging but does not by itself confirm the cause. [09:03–10:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QobBTNIIX04&t=543s), [10:05–11:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QobBTNIIX04&t=605s)
- **Filling every pause is different from preserving flow.** He acknowledges that running several projects in separate sessions can eliminate idle time, but argues that switching projects interrupts sustained attention and may increase stress. He also suggests writing code by hand for learning or enjoyment. His closing preference is to let automation create breathing room rather than automatically convert every saved minute into more work.

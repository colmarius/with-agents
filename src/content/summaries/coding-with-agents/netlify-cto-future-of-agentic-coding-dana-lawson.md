Dana Lawson, Netlify's CTO, joins Mayank Gupta on The West Coast Builders to discuss how coding agents change engineering workflows, code review, and version control. Lawson describes Netlify as an AI web development platform that handles CI/CD and infrastructure, and draws on earlier roles at developer-platform companies including New Relic, InVision, and GitHub. Her main argument is that teams should redesign workflows around agents instead of attaching agents to existing processes.

### Main takeaways

- **AI is widening Netlify's audience from developers to builders.** [02:57–05:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=177s): Lawson says that someone previously needed something already built before using a platform like Netlify, while now a good idea can be enough. She calls the lower barrier both unsettling for professional developers and empowering for everyone else. She still expects developers to have work.
- **Redesign the workflow instead of handing an agent the old one.** [04:00–05:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=240s): Lawson calls it a trap to have an agent repeat each step a person used to do. In her view, that approach produces a thousand tools whose behavior nobody understands. She calls agent orchestration immature and asks whether a workflow that pairs every agent with a person makes sense, compared with fewer, more capable agents that remove steps. [07:57–08:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=477s): she contrasts GitHub, where years of customer automation made workflow changes costly, with Netlify, where she says the team is willing to change workflows rather than bolt new technology onto them.
- **Agent sprawl can repeat service sprawl.** [07:57–10:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=477s): Lawson describes herself as a fan of monoliths. In earlier jobs, she says, splitting a monolith into 20 to 40 services was supposed to prevent large failures, but nobody watched each service closely enough, and the result was the opposite; she connects that outcome to telemetry and observability practices. She sees the same risk in separate SRE, coding-manager, and coordinator agents, which she says do not work reliably together yet. Her recommendation is to decide which agents are critical rather than adding one for every role.
- **Rollback can replace review only for work the team has decided to automate.** [05:02–07:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=302s): Lawson expects pull requests to disappear for some work, but not now. She would run autonomous loops only on very repeatable, predictable tasks. Her comparison is a human approving a 5,000-line pull request on a phone within five minutes: she says she would not trust that review more than automation. Asked who is accountable, she says the engineer still decides which changes are safe to run autonomously. A pull request assumes a person will review the change; for autonomous changes, she says the important control becomes rollback: merge to main and revert if it breaks. She does not describe the checks Netlify uses to make that safe.
- **Version control needs to serve new builders and faster agents.** [10:01–12:39](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=601s): Lawson does not call GitHub dead. She says it remains important to open source but must be willing to change, as Cursor did through several pivots. She notes that Git was designed to be distributed and that GitHub is hard to learn for people who are not developers; new builders still need revision control, which she says Netlify is simplifying. She expects that layer to become commoditized. Her argument for distribution is speed: agents that always call back to one central source-control location lose speed and autonomy. This is a design argument, not a measured comparison.
- **She wants a slowdown for safety, not for progress overall.** [12:39–14:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=759s): Lawson rejects a general slowdown. She cites Dario's remark that he does not fully understand how the models work, and says putting systems into production without understanding how they operate contradicts normal engineering practice. She also argues that not every country will pause, and mentions Chinese models as an example of work continuing elsewhere.
- **Cost may push teams toward local and open-weight models.** [14:34–16:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=874s): Lawson predicts that GPU shortages and spending will force some slowdown. She expects small language models running on local devices and open-weight models used where they fit. Her anecdotes are that her team stopped her from using an expensive model for a task and that CFOs now question AI spending, which she says has replaced infrastructure as the largest cost. Her statement that AI will help cure cancer is a forecast.
- **Her own work runs through agents more than an IDE.** [16:30–18:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=990s): Lawson says she moved from an older editor setup to VS Code and then Codespaces, and now rarely works in an IDE. She uses the CLI, Netlify itself, and agents: a Netlify agent invoked from Slack and an agent named Nina that she says knows Netlify's infrastructure. She keeps a personal "claw" agent on a laptop rather than at work because security is watching. She opens the code herself mainly when she is stuck. This is her personal workflow, not a description of Netlify's whole engineering team.
- **Leadership means accountability and human skills, not only technical depth.** [18:25–21:12](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=1105s): Lawson says her Army experience taught her rigor and how to unite people with different motives under one mission. She expects adults to do their jobs, separates reasons from excuses, and treats confused team members as a sign that she must lead better. [21:12–23:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dJBOrDIm6T0&t=1272s): her founder advice is to be directly involved in the product, since founders are now expected to be technical, and to take leadership training. She says founders often focus too much on the technical solution and too little on communication, culture, and the people involved.

### Practical interpretation

Editorial synthesis: Lawson's position on pull requests turns into a sorting question for each team. Which changes are repeatable and predictable enough to merge without human review, and can the team detect and roll back a failure quickly? The interview names rollback as the control but does not describe tests, monitoring, or other safeguards, so teams must establish those conditions themselves.

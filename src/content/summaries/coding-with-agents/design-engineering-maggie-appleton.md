Maggie Appleton discusses design engineering, her prototyping work at GitHub Next, and how coding agents change the work before implementation. Her practical approach combines paper sketches, browser prototypes, and agent-built controls for adjusting a design directly.

Her research prototypes also have a lower quality bar than production GitHub software.

### Key points

- **Design engineering connects user concepts to technical constraints.** Appleton describes product design as choosing understandable “nouns and verbs”: what objects users work with and what actions they can take. For agent tools, deciding whether sessions, plans, or groups of sessions are the main objects changes how users understand the product. Her definition of a design engineer adds knowledge of the underlying data and architecture, plus implementation work—not just polished animations. She notes that other design roles can legitimately focus more on user research than technical internals. [10:46–17:42](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=646s), [33:37–35:36](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=2017s)

- **Sketch before asking an agent to implement.** Paper lets her explore the shape of an interface quickly, before she can express it precisely in words. The sketch stays visible and easy to revise. She still uses Figma, but moves into browser prototypes early because font rendering, responsiveness, scrolling, and motion need to be judged in the medium users will experience. This is her workflow preference, not a claim that agents cannot accept images. [25:54–29:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=1554s), [38:43–39:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=2323s)

- **Have the agent build controls for the choices you need to make.** Her “jig” is a small, task-specific tool: sliders, color pickers, and other controls wired to a live prototype's variables. Instead of repeatedly describing a desired animation speed or contrast in chat, she changes it and sees the effect immediately, then commits the selected values. The interview describes this with a constellation animation. Such controls were possible to build manually before; agents make building a custom set more practical. [39:45–40:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=2385s), [47:40–50:37](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=2860s), [56:41–58:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=3401s)

- **Delegate implementation with an explicit verification loop.** Appleton reports asking an agent to implement a Figma design, use Playwright screenshots, and keep iterating until the result matches. This frees time for more ambitious prototypes, but she still supplies substantial design direction. These examples concern exploratory software that may be thrown away, not proof that the same process satisfies production maintenance or quality requirements. [19:26–20:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=1166s), [45:37–47:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=2737s)

- **Planning needs evidence appropriate to each decision.** Long sequences of multiple-choice questions can exhaust users until they simply accept the recommended answer. Appleton is prototyping decision cards with enough room for relevant evidence: a visual preview for border choices, or an architecture diagram for a structural choice. She proposes recording the responsible human and the information available at the time so teams can later recover the rationale. These are design explorations, not a finished planning system. [21:17–24:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=1277s)

- **Faster individual coding does not establish team agreement.** Appleton argues that teams still need to agree on feature scope, interfaces, and technical changes before handing work to agents. Shared sessions help expose work, but do not themselves settle decisions. GitHub Next's ACE prototype combined a Slack-like workspace with shared sandboxes and review tools; she says turning the whole system into a product proved too ambitious for the small team, so they are exploring smaller pieces and related questions. [1:00:32–1:07:00](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=3632s)

- **Familiar interfaces may work better than novel AI interfaces.** At Elicit, the team tried canvases and cards for extracting data from research papers, but users repeatedly asked for a table. Appleton's explanation is familiarity and lower cognitive load: the team could add capabilities within and around the table rather than require a new interaction model. Generating many polished alternatives also does not replace checking whether users understand a new product concept. [50:37–52:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=3037s), [53:47–56:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=3227s)

- **Do not generalize from one impressive model result.** Her phrase “capability gaslighting” describes the misleading confidence she feels when a model excels at one task and then fails at another. She reports that design rules in skills still need hands-on correction. These are accounts of uneven performance and unmet preferences, not benchmark measurements or evidence of intentional deception. [58:28–1:00:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=3508s), [1:08:03–1:09:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KZSzF0KEFRg&t=4083s)

- **Small community software needs sound foundations.** Appleton extends personal “home-cooked software” to “barefoot developers”: technically inclined community members building small tools for local needs. She argues that these builders need frameworks with good security and data persistence, and favors local-first foundations that keep data under users' control. This is a proposal for supporting less experienced builders, not a claim that generated apps are safe without engineering safeguards.

Joyce Er and Kent C. Dodds discuss how engineers develop product judgment when AI makes writing code cheaper. Most of the conversation covers product practices that predate coding agents: end-to-end feature ownership, dogfooding, customer exposure, cross-team relationships, and understanding how the business sells. AI comes up mainly as the reason Er thinks judgment now matters more.

This summary is based on the English auto-generated captions. Er introduces herself as a former software engineer on VS Code at Microsoft for five years and now a product manager at Cerebras, which makes wafer-scale chips for AI inference; her surname comes from the video title. Descriptions of VS Code and Cerebras practices are her first-party accounts, and claims about where software engineering is heading are her opinions.

### Key points

- **Er argues that judgment is replacing implementation as the main work.** She says engineering "as we knew it is sort of dead": typing and designing code once took most of an engineer's time, and AI has shrunk that part. What remains is deciding why something is built and for whom. As a product manager, she now builds tools for herself between meetings that she previously could not justify. This is her view of the field, not a measured change. [02:50–03:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=170s), [03:55–04:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=235s)
- **On VS Code, engineers owned what shipped, not just how it was built.** Er says each engineer, working with a product manager, owned the end-to-end definition of a feature and why it worked that way. Because teammates used the tool daily, engineers got fast feedback and built their own judgment. She credits dogfooding, meaning using your own product every day, for that tight feedback loop and says Cerebras's product team does the same. One consequence is that nobody tells you what to build, so you need your own opinions and must triage more ideas than you have time for. [04:57–06:01](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=297s), [06:01–08:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=361s)
- **Cheap execution makes ownership more important.** Er warns that building with AI can produce "UX slop": many features added to a product without making it coherent or simple. Engineers who use the product daily and answer for their users' success are positioned to prevent that. She adds that as execution gets cheaper, judgment is how engineers keep their value. [08:05–09:05](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=485s), [09:05–10:02](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=545s)
- **A friction log builds judgment from your own use.** Er's main recommendation is a running list of the "paper cuts" you hit while using any product, not only your own. Her suggested routine is five minutes at the end of each day writing down what took too long or confused you. Asking why something is confusing and how you would change it gives you practice judging user experience. [10:54–11:45](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=654s), [11:45–12:40](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=705s), [34:27–35:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=2067s)
- **How much design discipline to apply depends on the project's stage.** Er says intuition for designing systems comes from seeing features land differently than intended, get deprecated, or lose relevance when markets shift. During early prototyping, she finds AI most useful: an agent can refactor the whole application or discard the database schema and start over. Once you commit to maintaining a system, that past experience matters again. [12:40–13:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=760s), [13:43–14:44](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=823s)
- **Experience helps, but some of it must be unlearned.** Er calls cutting junior hiring a short-term cost saving and says her company hires juniors. She also says experienced engineers must decide which of their assumptions to discard as AI has moved from code completion to building whole applications. In her opinion, the fewer assumptions you hold about what AI can do, the better. Dodds compares this to teaching React Hooks, which newcomers learned faster than experienced React developers. [13:43–15:35](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=823s), [15:35–17:25](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=935s)
- **Rituals can create ownership.** In VS Code's daily stand-up, Er says each engineer had a five-minute slot to explain a feature, the problem it solved, and to demonstrate it. Colleagues then gave blunt feedback on everything from button padding to whether the feature fit the product. A lighter alternative is a bug bash or an internal dogfooding program. Dodds adds that failing to persuade colleagues to use a feature behind a feature flag signals friction worth investigating. [17:25–18:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=1045s), [18:30–20:50](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=1110s)
- **When you cannot dogfood, watch real users.** For software such as surgical tools, which engineers cannot easily use themselves, Er suggests asking a product manager to let you sit in on customer calls. On those calls she saw users struggle with crowded menus and skip important modals out of habit. Industry meetups and conferences where customers gather are another option. [20:19–21:07](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=1219s), [21:07–22:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=1267s)
- **Cross-team dependencies need early agreement and relationships.** Er says VS Code engineers rarely faced this problem because they owned vertical slices. Where other teams are involved, she recommends bringing them together at the start and motivating the work with a broken customer experience or a business metric the company cares about. Understanding the partner team's priorities first also helps. She calls relationship building one of the skills hardest to automate. Dodds adds that relationships reveal existing building blocks, so you avoid rebuilding them. Er's habit is to have lunch with engineers working two or three steps away in the stack. [22:08–25:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=1328s), [25:06–28:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=1506s)
- **Learn how the business sells.** Er's second recommendation is to read a sales book. Salespeople at Cerebras recommended *The Qualified Sales Leader*, which covers enterprise sales. She argues that engineers building products sold to businesses, rather than bought with a credit card, should understand how their work creates business value for the company. [35:28–36:27](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=2128s)

### Relevance to coding agents

The coding-agent content is limited to Er's opinions about cheaper implementation, UX slop, AI-assisted prototyping, and skills that resist automation. She also mentions that many of Cerebras's customers are in the coding space and that she uses their products herself. [30:57–32:49](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pqtUVssiPRc&t=1857s) The friction log, stand-up demos, customer calls, and sales reading are general product-engineering practices; the conversation gives no example of applying them to agent-built software.
